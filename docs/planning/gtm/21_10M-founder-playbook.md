# GTM Tactics: "$10M/Year Founder Shares His Exact Playbook (He Did It Twice)"
Source: https://youtu.be/t-jDFgy1U2Y
Channel: Florian Darroman (@asyncr0ne)

Guest: Andrew Gazdecki — built Business Apps to $10M ARR then 8-figure exit, then built Acquire.com (leading startup acquisition marketplace, $1B+ in closed deals).

---

## Extracted Tactics

---

**[Ride a Technology Shift]**
- **What they did**: Business Apps launched right when the iPhone came out. "Push notifications were kind of like magic — how'd you do this?" The product was simple (drag-and-drop mobile app builder for small businesses) but the timing with smartphones was perfect. "It just exploded. It was the right place, right time."
- **Why it worked**: Technology shifts create new possibility spaces where incumbents haven't built yet. Small businesses needed mobile apps but couldn't afford $50K custom development. The shift made previously impossible things suddenly possible and affordable.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO rides the AI agent shift. Just as smartphones made mobile ordering possible for pizza shops, AI agents make autonomous collaboration possible for knowledge workers. The shift: people are already using AI agents daily (Claude Code, Codex, Hermes) but have no secure way to connect them. The "push notification magic" equivalent is two agents securely introducing themselves and collaborating without human pasting into Slack.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

---

**[Distribution Company That Sells Software]**
- **What they did**: "I would still tell the company internally — I tell everybody that we were a distribution company. Even though we sold software, our main focus is distribution." Business Apps focused obsessively on how to get the product into customers' hands rather than on feature development.
- **Why it worked**: As products become easier to build (especially with AI), the competitive moat shifts entirely to distribution. Building a great product is necessary but insufficient. The company that wins is the one that reaches the customer first and most efficiently.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO must think of itself as a distribution company that happens to sell a cryptographic trust layer. The core question is not "how do we improve the protocol" but "how do we get it installed on the next 1,000 machines." The MCP install command (`claude mcp add cello -- npx @cello-protocol/connect`) IS the product from a distribution perspective. Every friction point in that chain is a distribution failure.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Reseller/Channel Partner Model]**
- **What they did**: Partnered with digital agencies (web design companies) worldwide. Let them white-label the software and sell mobile apps to their existing clients. "That allowed us to sell tens of thousands of mobile apps all across the world." Translated the application into 30+ languages. Without this model, they would have needed massive VC to go direct to small businesses.
- **Why it worked**: Agencies already had trust relationships with small businesses. Instead of cold-calling restaurant owners (brutal), they piggybacked on existing trusted relationships. Each agency partner brought dozens of clients. The economics worked because partners did the selling.
- **CELLO application**: The equivalent channel partners for CELLO are AI agent platforms and tool builders. If OpenClaw, Hermes, or a Claude Code extension ecosystem partner pre-installs or recommends CELLO as the identity/trust layer, each platform brings its entire user base. Also: consultants and agencies that set up AI workflows for clients could bundle CELLO as the security/identity layer. "We set up your agents AND we make sure they can talk to each other securely" is a natural upsell for any AI implementation consultant.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Press as Primary Distribution (Pre-Social Era)]**
- **What they did**: "We were in TechCrunch a ton. I was a guest author there, Venture Beat, New York Times, Wall Street Journal." Were "maniacal about press" — pitched every partnership, product update, and milestone as an exclusive. When a writer said no, they'd pitch again a month later.
- **Why it worked**: Before Twitter/LinkedIn were effective distribution channels, press was the only way to reach potential customers at scale. They treated press pitching with the same persistence as sales.
- **CELLO application**: The modern equivalent is not press (which has declined) but rather developer/AI-focused content platforms. For CELLO: guest posts on AI engineering blogs, deep-dives on Hacker News, interviews on AI-focused podcasts (Latent Space, AI Engineering, etc.), and being the go-to expert source when journalists write about "AI agent security" or "AI trust." The principle is the same: be maniacal about getting into the information channels your ICP already reads.
- **Category**: `content-format`

**[Brand Story That Goes Beyond the Product]**
- **What they did**: "I try to tell a story that really resonates and kind of goes beyond what we do and what we sell." At Business Apps: championing small businesses competing against mega brands. At Acquire: championing bootstrap founders who don't need to build a billion-dollar company to succeed. People "root for your company regardless of if they're going to be a customer or not."
- **Why it worked**: People remember stories, not stats. A brand story creates emotional buy-in from an audience larger than your customer base. That audience amplifies, refers, and champions you. The story makes the company feel like a movement, not just a product.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO's brand story is NOT about cryptography. It's about **autonomy and sovereignty** — your AI agents are yours, they answer to you, and they should be able to collaborate with anyone without asking permission from a platform gatekeeper. The "champion" narrative: solo operators, small teams, and independent consultants deserve the same secure AI infrastructure as big enterprises. "You don't need to trust a platform with your agent's communications." Make people root for decentralized, sovereign AI agent identity — even if they're not yet a user.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Innovate on Distribution as Much as Product]**
- **What they did**: "Unique ways to attract customers — that should be the main thing." Cited a compliance company using a hilarious meme account on Twitter, and Whisper Flow using a novel influencer strategy where when one post goes viral, they tell all other influencers to make similar content.
- **Why it worked**: Mainstream distribution channels (paid ads, cold email, generic social) are saturated and expensive. "Marketers ruin everything." The companies that win find a distribution channel that nobody else is using yet, giving them outsized returns before the channel becomes crowded.
- **CELLO application**: For CELLO, the novel distribution angle could be: (1) Live public agent-to-agent sessions that people can watch in real-time (like a stream of two agents collaborating, showing the trust handshake, the session seal — making the invisible visible), (2) A "CELLO verified" badge system that agents can display, creating social proof pressure, (3) Agent directories where CELLO-enabled agents can discover each other — making CELLO the DNS of the agent world, (4) Integrating directly into the Claude Code / MCP ecosystem updates and changelogs.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Start a Newsletter/Podcast to Attract Ideas and Network]**
- **What they did**: Andrew's specific advice for starting over in 2026: "I'd probably start a newsletter. I'd start interviewing a bunch of AI experts. I'd narrow in on the industry." Before Business Apps, he ran a job board connecting mobile developers with businesses — "an idea petri dish where I could attract ideas to it."
- **Why it worked**: Creating content in an industry gives you three things simultaneously: a network of experts, pattern recognition for pain points, and an audience that already trusts you when you launch. The job board literally showed him the Business Apps idea (people posting the same $100K job repeatedly).
- **CELLO application**: Andre already has content from building CELLO (the design docs, discussion logs, architectural decisions). Publishing a "Building Agent Trust Infrastructure in Public" series — covering real decisions about threshold cryptography, P2P identity, prompt injection defense — positions CELLO as the thought leader. Each piece of content attracts the exact ICP: people thinking deeply about AI agent collaboration who eventually need a solution. The content IS the top of the funnel.
- **Category**: `content-format`

**[Niche Down Aggressively]**
- **What they did**: Andrew's biggest regret with Business Apps: "We went super broad. Could we go very specific, meaning like only serving pizza shops that have a bunch of items and want ordering?" His 2026 advice: take a big clunky system (Workday, Salesforce) and rebuild a specific boring B2B slice with AI.
- **Why it worked**: Narrow focus means you can dominate a specific pain point, build word-of-mouth within a tight community, and avoid competing with generalist incumbents on every front simultaneously. Specificity also makes distribution easier — you know exactly where your customers congregate.
- **CELLO application**: Rather than launching CELLO for "all AI agents everywhere," niche down to a specific wedge use case. The solo multi-agent use case (connecting your own Claude Code to your own Hermes/AWS agent) is already the narrowest wedge. But even within friend-to-friend: target a specific collaboration pattern first. Example: "AI agents that share research findings between consultants" or "Developer agents that can securely hand off code reviews to a colleague's agent." One use case, nailed perfectly, creates the beachhead.
- **Category**: `launch-tactic`

**[Bias for Action + Long Time Horizon]**
- **What they did**: The two separators for founders who exit at $10M: "Bias for action — one way or another they were able to execute on the vision" and "sticking with it for a long period of time." Average time to a $10M exit: 3-4 years. Some businesses on Acquire.com have been around 20 years.
- **Why it worked**: Compounding requires time. "If you're growing at 20, 30, 40, 50%, you do that for five years, you're going to have a pretty big business." Years 1-3 are building product/brand/team. Years 4-5 are where things "really pick up." Giving up in year 2 means missing the compounding phase.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is deep infrastructure (cryptographic identity, threshold signatures, hash chains). This is not a quick-flip product. The trust required for adoption compounds over time — each successful session, each sealed receipt, each endorsement strengthens the network. Plan for a 3-5 year horizon. The protocol's value increases non-linearly as more agents join. The bias for action means: ship the wedge now (solo multi-agent), don't wait for perfection.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Mentorship Through Doing Something Interesting]**
- **What they did**: Met his mentor (Christian Freeland, who sold Build.com for $50M) by entering an entrepreneurship contest at college with an already-built MVP. "If I just went up to him and said 'Hey, I want you to mentor me, I have some ideas' — he would have said get lost, kid." The mentor naturally appeared because Andrew was already executing.
- **Why it worked**: Accomplished people want to help other people who are already in motion. A live project with real traction is infinitely more interesting than a pitch deck. The MVP demonstrated seriousness and created a natural conversation opener.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is already built, open source, and functioning. This is exactly the state that attracts advisor-caliber people. When approaching potential advisors, partners, or early advocates — lead with the working product, the live sessions, the sealed receipts. "We have two agents communicating over CELLO right now, here's a transcript" is immeasurably more compelling than "we're building a trust layer for agents."
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[CEO Peer Group / Mastermind]**
- **What they did**: Paid $12,000/year for an invite-only CEO peer group (10X CEO). Six CEOs meet quarterly, share numbers, rate team members, rate themselves against top priorities. Had a moderator who was a successful founder sharing frameworks.
- **Why it worked**: Accountability, frameworks, and peer-level feedback. "Great CEOs and great founders — they weren't born knowing everything. They made a concerted effort to go out and learn." The investment felt expensive but justified: "If you spend $100K on something like this and it grows your business by 10%, that could be potentially millions."
- **CELLO application**: For a solo founder pre-launch, the equivalent is a small group of other solo founders building in the AI infrastructure space. Not a paid mastermind necessarily, but a tight accountability group. Share numbers monthly, challenge each other's priorities. Also: the open-source community around CELLO can serve this function — early contributors and advocates who challenge assumptions and hold the project accountable to shipping.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Monthly Founder Journal]**
- **What they did**: Writes a 60-second journal entry once a month: what happened, top worries, three things grateful for. When writing next month's entry, reviews last month's worries — which are usually resolved. "It helps with the mental aspect... it's a proof stacking that everything will always be right."
- **Why it worked**: Entrepreneurship is psychologically brutal. The journal creates empirical evidence that problems get solved, reducing anxiety about current problems. It's cognitive reframing through personal data.
- **CELLO application**: Andre already maintains build journals and discussion logs. The GTM equivalent: a monthly "CELLO traction journal" — installs this month, sessions run, new contacts formed, pain points heard from early users. Review last month's worries ("will anyone install this?" "does the security story resonate?") and note which resolved. Useful for investor conversations and self-assessment, but primarily for founder mental health during the long pre-traction phase.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[B2B SaaS with Low Churn Is the Optimal Vehicle]**
- **What they did**: From Acquire.com data across $1B in transactions: "B2B by far." B2C churn can hit 7-10% monthly, which "really slows down growth and slows down your valuation." 70% of acquisitions are SaaS. Bootstrap beats VC (95% of sales are bootstrapped companies; VC-backed sales are "generally not the happiest story").
- **Why it worked**: B2B SaaS has predictable revenue, lower churn, higher willingness to pay, and cleaner metrics. Buyers want businesses that compound reliably. Bootstrap means the founder keeps all the upside.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is already structured as B2B infrastructure (agents serving operators who are professionals/businesses). The key insight: ensure the pricing model creates sticky, recurring revenue — not one-time installs. This could be: monthly active agent subscriptions, per-session fees, or a tiered plan based on number of contacts/endorsements. The "churn" equivalent for CELLO: ensure agents stay registered and active (not just installed once and forgotten). Active sessions and growing contact lists are the retention mechanism.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Competition in the Sales Process Drives Multiples]**
- **What they did**: "Most surprising: just how important competition is in the acquisition process." Businesses with one buyer sell at 0.5x revenue. Multiple competing buyers drive price up dramatically. "If you have one buyer, you have no buyers."
- **Why it worked**: Without alternatives, a buyer dictates terms. Competition creates urgency and FOMO. The tactical move: when you receive one offer, let other buyers know, negotiate upward.
- **CELLO application**: While exit strategy is premature, the principle applies to partnerships and early adoption. Don't be dependent on a single platform (Claude Code) for distribution. Ensure CELLO works with multiple AI agent platforms (OpenClaw, Hermes, Codex, custom agents). If one platform sees competitors adopting CELLO, it creates urgency to integrate. Same applies to potential investors/acquirers down the line: demonstrate that multiple platforms depend on CELLO, not just one.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Document Everything From Day One]**
- **What they did**: Best acquisition process Andrew ever saw: the founder recorded a Loom video every time an employee asked a question. Built a complete data room documenting every aspect of the business. "He had a clear operating manual." Minimum: good bookkeeper, P&L, and 5-10 minute Looms covering how marketing, support, and operations work.
- **Why it worked**: Documentation de-risks the business for buyers (and for scaling). It proves the business isn't a black box that only the founder can run. It also enables delegation — which is required for the founder to step back (itself a signal of business maturity).
- **CELLO application**: CELLO already has extensive documentation (Obsidian vault, discussion logs, ADRs, build journals). This is a strength for open-source credibility AND future acquisition/investment readiness. Continue the pattern: document operational runbooks (how to deploy, how to add a node, how to handle a security incident). For the product itself: ensure CELLO-enabled agents can explain their trust state and session history clearly — documentation as a product feature.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Lean Team, Capital Efficient]**
- **What they did**: Businesses that sell for $10M+ are "highly efficient, capital efficient — the bare minimum number of people required." Solo founders and co-founder duos both work. Large teams (50+) are rare in successful exits. At Business Apps, the 75-person team was built over years by hiring offshore first (China, Ukraine, Philippines, South America).
- **Why it worked**: Lean teams mean higher margins, faster decisions, and cleaner cap tables. Buyers pay multiples of profit, not revenue — so efficiency directly multiplies exit value. Offshore hiring gave enterprise-level capabilities at startup-level costs.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is solo-founder, AI-assisted development (Claude Code building the product). This is already maximally lean. The playbook says this is exactly right for the stage. When it's time to add people: prioritize roles that directly enable distribution (developer advocate, community manager) over additional engineering — the AI-assisted development model means one engineer (Andre + Claude Code) can maintain the protocol.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Avoid Saturated Distribution Channels]**
- **What they did**: "I don't think just doing a bunch of paid ads or generic social marketing or cold emailing a bunch of people — I don't think that necessarily works as well as it used to. Mainstream distribution channels should be avoided." Cited the saying "marketers ruin everything."
- **Why it worked**: When a channel gets crowded, cost per acquisition rises and effectiveness drops. The founders who win find underpriced attention (channels where their ICP hangs out but competitors haven't arrived yet).
- **CELLO application**: For CELLO's ICP (people using AI agents who need collaboration), the underpriced channels right now are: (1) MCP server directories and Claude Code ecosystem lists (tiny but perfectly targeted), (2) AI agent Discord servers and communities where practitioners share workflows, (3) Specific subreddits and forums about AI automation, (4) Direct integration with other open-source agent projects. What to AVOID: generic "AI" content on LinkedIn, broad developer marketing, paid ads on Google for "AI agent security." Too broad, too expensive, wrong audience.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Pitch Without Raising — Always Be Pitching]**
- **What they did**: "You don't have to pitch investors, but you still got to pitch customers, employees, the market as a whole, press, going on podcasts... you're always kind of pitching what you do." The pitch IS the daily marketing strategy.
- **Why it worked**: A crisp, compelling pitch makes everything else easier — content creation, partnership conversations, user onboarding, hiring. It's not a fundraising artifact; it's an operational communication tool used daily.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO needs a 30-second pitch that doesn't mention cryptography, FROST signatures, or hash chains. Something like: "Your AI agents already talk to tools. CELLO lets them talk to each other — securely, privately, and with receipts. Install it in one command. Your Claude Code can now collaborate with your friend's agent." Practice this pitch in every context: README, website hero, podcast appearances, community posts, DMs to potential early users.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

## Foundations / Prerequisites

The following must be in place before tactical execution matters:

1. **Technology shift alignment** — CELLO must clearly articulate how it rides the AI agent shift (agents need to collaborate but currently can't do so securely). Every piece of marketing should make the shift feel inevitable and CELLO feel like the obvious solution.

2. **Distribution-first mindset** — Internalize that CELLO is a distribution company that happens to build cryptographic infrastructure. Every decision filters through "does this get CELLO installed on more machines?"

3. **Brand story beyond the product** — Define the movement narrative: sovereign AI agents, no platform gatekeepers, your agents answer to you. People should be able to root for CELLO even before they use it.

4. **Crisp 30-second pitch** — No jargon, no crypto terminology. Lead with the pain (agents can't collaborate), the solution (one-command install), and the proof (sealed receipts, trust signals).

5. **Channel partner strategy** — Identify 3-5 potential channel partners (AI agent platforms, automation consultants, MCP ecosystem projects) who could distribute CELLO to their existing users.

6. **Lean operations** — Solo founder + AI-assisted development is already optimal. First hires should be distribution-focused (developer advocate, community), not engineering.

7. **Niche wedge defined** — Solo multi-agent (connecting your own agents) is the beachhead. Nail this completely before expanding to friend-to-friend or broader network effects.

8. **Monthly traction journal** — Track installs, active sessions, contacts formed, pain points heard. Review monthly to build evidence that problems resolve and traction compounds.

9. **Novel distribution channel** — Find the underpriced attention channel where CELLO's exact ICP congregates but competitors haven't arrived. Currently: MCP directories, AI agent communities, open-source agent project integrations.

10. **Documentation as asset** — Continue the extensive documentation practice. It serves triple duty: open-source credibility, operational clarity, and future acquisition/investment readiness.
