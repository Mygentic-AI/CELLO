Florian Darroman synthesizes five recurring patterns from asking 20 active founders (revenue range $100K–$10M/year) the same question: "What's the best way to find a $1M startup idea?" The patterns span the full pre-launch arc: find a paying buyer before writing code (David Park, Nadav), validate that a market exists by checking whether competitors are already making money (Ain, Outrank $200K/month), exploit innate unsolvable human desires for B2C virality (David Atias), wrap emerging tech in accessible form before the official mobile launch (Lots, $500K in exits), and mine niche newsletters to identify pain and reach pre-qualified buyers. The video is pitched at early-stage founders who have not yet committed to an idea, but the tactics — especially trend-riding, newsletter sponsorship, and pre-sell validation — apply directly to a built product still finding its distribution strategy.

## Extracted Tactics

---

**[Talk to People Around You — Problem Mining via Conversation]**
- **What they did**: David Park (Jenny AI, $10M/year) says to ask people in your network specific questions: "What's the role you're trying to hire for? What's something you wish they did better? What's a recurring problem in your company?" When someone names a problem, follow up with: "If I solve that for you, will you pay me $100-$1,000 a month?"
- **Why it worked**: 9 out of 10 people are just complaining. But 1 out of 10 will say yes — and that's your paying customer identified before any code exists. The specificity of the question ("will you pay") filters complainers from genuine buyers instantly.
- **CELLO application**: Ask people who use multiple AI agents (consultants running Claude Code + Hermes, GTM pros with parallel agents): "What breaks when your agents need to work together? What do you paste into Slack that an agent should have sent directly?" The ones who say "I would pay to stop doing that" are your first waitlist converts. Target the collaboration friction, not the crypto mechanism.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

---

**[Pre-sell Before Building — Payment as Ultimate Validation]**
- **What they did**: Nadav asked potential customers to pay before he even built the product. "They got $20,000 and they used that money to build the product to solve the problem." Payment before product = the strongest signal that the problem is real.
- **Why it worked**: Money is the only honest signal. Verbal interest is free. A check requires genuine pain. The $20K also funded the build — zero financial risk.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is already built, but the principle applies to premium tiers or enterprise features. Offer a "founding operator" tier: pay $X now, get white-glove setup + priority features + lifetime pricing. Even $50/year pre-commitments from 20 people would validate the paid tier and fund infrastructure costs. For the waitlist, ask: "Would you pay $10/month to never paste between agents again?" — the yes-rate tells you everything.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Find Your First Customer, Then Find Their Clones]**
- **What they did**: After the first person pays, "you can go back to this first person you asked the question to and find people that are similar to this person because you know for a fact that these people will actually be your best customer." Your first buyer defines your ICP retroactively.
- **Why it worked**: The first buyer is a living, breathing profile of your ideal customer — their role, company size, workflow, pain threshold. Finding more of them is a targeting exercise, not a discovery exercise.
- **CELLO application**: Once the first external operator installs CELLO and uses it daily (beyond Andre's solo use), study that person obsessively. What do they do? Which agents do they connect? What was their aha moment? Then find communities where people with that same profile hang out — likely AI-power-user communities, automation-focused Slack groups, or indie hacker circles where multi-agent workflows are common.
- **Category**: `launch-tactic`

**[Keep an Idea Ocean — Systematic Idea Collection]**
- **What they did**: Ain (Outrank, $200K/month) maintains an "ocean dock" where he collects business ideas. List 10 ideas — trending topics or personal problems. For each, research current market and competitors. Try competitor products, find gaps.
- **Why it worked**: Systematic idea evaluation prevents premature commitment. Checking what exists prevents building in a vacuum. Trying competitor products reveals UX gaps that become your differentiator.
- **CELLO application**: Not about finding CELLO's idea (it exists), but about competitive positioning. Systematically try every "agent collaboration" tool that exists: shared context protocols, MCP routers, agent-to-agent frameworks. Document what each does poorly. CELLO's differentiator (cryptographic trust, no central platform, P2P) emerges from showing what competitors lack — not from describing CELLO's features in isolation.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[If No One Built It, Don't Build It — Market Existence as Validation]**
- **What they did**: Ain's explicit warning: "If an idea has no solution, if no one built a product for it, don't build it. Because validating an idea is hard. But if someone already built a product and makes money with it, you know for a fact that there is money to make."
- **Why it worked**: Existing competitors are proof of demand. Building where no market exists means you must both educate the market AND build the product — two enormous bets at once.
- **CELLO application**: The "agent collaboration" space has adjacent competitors (shared context, tool-use protocols, multi-agent orchestration). That validates demand. CELLO's P2P trust layer is novel, but the NEED it addresses (agents working together) is already being solved poorly by others — that's good. Position against existing solutions (pasting into Slack, shared API keys, centralized agent platforms) rather than claiming to invent a new category from scratch.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Build v1 in One to Two Months with Creativity Based on Competitor]**
- **What they did**: Ain says to "build the first version in one to two months maximum with creativity based on competitor. You take your competitor and see what feature you can add that will make your product different from this one."
- **Why it worked**: Speed prevents over-engineering. Competitor analysis ensures you're building something the market already understands, but with a clear "why switch" differentiator.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO is already built — but the lesson applies to positioning and messaging. The "creativity based on competitor" means: show exactly what existing solutions do (centralized platforms, shared API keys, pasting into Slack) and demonstrate the one thing CELLO adds that they cannot (cryptographic trust, P2P privacy, sealed receipts). The differentiator IS the wedge.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Make Your Life Interesting to Encounter New Problems]**
- **What they did**: Peter Level's advice from his book "Make": "If you don't have any problem to solve, your life is too boring. Make it interesting." Go outside, learn instruments, do triathlons, live in a new country (John Yungfuk went to Japan — "discovering a culture totally different from yours will unlock creativity in your brain").
- **Why it worked**: Novel experiences create novel problems. You can't build solutions for problems you've never encountered. Exposure diversity = problem diversity.
- **CELLO application**: Andre already lives this (multi-country, multi-agent workflows daily). The tactic for CELLO is: put yourself in unfamiliar collaboration contexts where agent-to-agent communication breaks down in new ways. Join a hackathon team where everyone uses different AI agents. Consult for someone in a different industry. Each new context reveals new failure modes that become features or marketing stories.
- **Category**: `prerequisite`

**[Innate Human Insecurities — Problems That Cannot Be Fully Solved]**
- **What they did**: David Atias (Stopper app) identifies problems "that can't be solved but people desperately want solved." Examples: Taller (stretching to grow taller), Penometer (scanning for data). "The more weird the concept is, the more viral it goes."
- **Why it worked**: Unsolvable problems create infinite retention — the customer never "graduates." And the weirdness makes it inherently shareable content. People screenshot and share bizarre apps.
- **CELLO application**: Less directly applicable to B2B infrastructure. However, the "unsolvable" angle maps to: AI trust is an unsolvable problem in the sense that you can never fully trust a counterparty's agent. You can only accumulate evidence. This means CELLO's trust signals never "complete" — there's always more reputation to build, more endorsements to earn. That's retention by design. For viral content: the weird/fascinating angle is "your AI agent has its own cryptographic identity and reputation score" — that's inherently shareable as a concept.
- **Category**: `viral-mechanic`

**[Find Expensive Physical Procedures, Create Cheaper AI Alternatives]**
- **What they did**: Lots' framework: "Identify why people want the outcome but avoid the procedure because it's dangerous, expensive, or risky. Then create an app to solve this problem." Example: leg-lengthening surgery costs $75K+ and is dangerous. Taller app sells stretching for $39/year.
- **Why it worked**: The gap between "wants outcome" and "won't do the procedure" is enormous. Any alternative in that gap — even an imperfect one — captures demand. The price anchoring ($39/year vs $75K surgery) makes the purchase feel like nothing.
- **CELLO application**: The "expensive physical procedure" equivalent for CELLO: legal contracts for AI agent interactions, manual audit trails, hiring security consultants to verify agent communications, SOC2 compliance for every agent integration. These are the expensive/painful current approaches. CELLO gives you cryptographic proof for $10/month instead of $50K/year in compliance costs. Frame it: "Sealed receipts = automatic audit trail. No lawyer needed."
- **Category**: `launch-tactic`

**[Sell for $39/year — Low Price Point for B2C with Weird Virality]**
- **What they did**: Lots' pricing model for mobile apps solving insecurity problems: "$39 a year and people will pay for it." Distribution: "Once you get this kind of weird ideas, the virality will be way easier on social media. You can do any video and it will get viral."
- **Why it worked**: Low annual price removes purchase friction entirely. Combined with inherently shareable concepts, the CAC approaches zero because content does the distribution work.
- **CELLO application**: CELLO's pricing hasn't been set, but the principle is: the lowest tier should be priced at "impulse buy" level ($5-10/month or $39-49/year) so that the decision to try it is trivial. The viral content angle: demo videos of "watch two AI agents negotiate, verify each other, and seal a session" are inherently fascinating to the AI-user audience. The weirdness of agents having their own identity IS the viral hook.
- **Category**: `product-led-growth`

**[Trend Wrapping — Wrap New Tech in Accessible Form Before Official Launch]**
- **What they did**: Lots wraps new technology launches into mobile apps before the official mobile version exists. "When a new technology launches like ChatGPT, Sora, Midjourney — they require desktop or Discord to access initially." He wrapped an AI music app in 4 days, hit #1 in App Store, exited 4 apps for $500K total.
- **Why it worked**: New tech creates massive demand but limited accessibility. Wrapping it in a more accessible form (mobile app) captures the demand wave. Speed is everything — you must ship in days, not weeks.
- **CELLO application**: When new AI agent frameworks launch (new Claude features, new MCP capabilities, new agent platforms), be the first to show "here's how you connect it to CELLO." Every new agent platform launch is a distribution opportunity: write the integration guide, ship the adapter, publish the "connect X to CELLO in 5 minutes" tutorial on Day 1. Be the trust layer that rides every new agent wave. Specifically: when Claude adds new agentic features, when Codex ships updates, when new MCP-compatible tools appear — have a CELLO integration ready immediately.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Newsletter Problem Mining — Subscribe, Identify Pain, Build, Sponsor]**
- **What they did**: Florian's own technique: Subscribe to newsletters in your target niche (e.g., Justin Welsh's 100K+ subscriber newsletter). Read every issue. Identify recurring problems the newsletter author talks about. Build a solution. Then pay for sponsorship in that same newsletter to reach the exact audience with the exact pain point.
- **Why it worked**: Newsletter authors are professional pain-point identifiers — they study their audience daily. Their subscriber list IS a curated list of ideal customers. Sponsoring the newsletter puts your solution in front of pre-qualified buyers who already recognize the problem.
- **CELLO application**: Identify newsletters read by AI power users: AI-focused newsletters (Ben's Bites, The Neuron, Superhuman AI), indie hacker newsletters, automation-focused newsletters. Read them for recurring complaints about agent collaboration, context sharing, trust, or security. Then sponsor those newsletters with a message that speaks directly to the pain they've been discussing. Even better: build a relationship with the newsletter author and offer them CELLO for their own agents — they become a case study AND a distribution channel.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

**[Partner with the Newsletter Owner — Equity/Revenue Share for Distribution]**
- **What they did**: "Contact the person behind the newsletter and ask for a partnership like influencer marketing or give this person a share of your products so he can also push it." Example: Danco had people contact him to build agencies or SaaS tools for his audience — partnerships where the influencer has skin in the game.
- **Why it worked**: When the influencer owns a piece of the solution, their incentives align permanently. They don't just mention you once — they actively promote because it's their revenue too. This turns a one-time sponsorship into ongoing, authentic distribution.
- **CELLO application**: Identify AI thought leaders or community builders (people running AI automation communities, agent-focused Discord servers, AI consultancies). Offer them: "You get X% of revenue from everyone you bring in" or "You become a founding operator with equity-adjacent benefits." For CELLO specifically: offer prominent AI creators early access + affiliate revenue for referrals. Their endorsement (especially if they use CELLO visibly in their own agent workflows) is worth more than any ad spend.
- **Category**: `partnership`

**[Speed Is Everything in Trend-Based Markets]**
- **What they did**: Lots emphasizes: "You have to be really fast. If you see a trend, if you see Sora, the new Sora arriving online, you need to wrap it as fast as possible." He built apps in 4 days to capture trend waves.
- **Why it worked**: First-mover advantage in trend wrapping is absolute. The window closes when the official version launches or when 10 copycats flood the market. Days matter, not weeks.
- **CELLO application**: When a major AI agent announcement happens (new Claude model, new agentic capability, new competitor to MCP, new enterprise agent deployment), publish content within 24-48 hours showing how CELLO integrates with or solves a problem created by the announcement. Speed of content > quality of content for trend-riding. A rough "here's how to connect X to CELLO" video on Day 1 beats a polished tutorial on Day 14.
- **Category**: `ongoing-distribution`

## Foundations / Prerequisites

The 20-founder synthesis reveals five patterns, of which three are prerequisites that CELLO has already satisfied or can quickly satisfy:

1. **Problem validation through payment** (Techniques 1 & 2): CELLO's problem is validated by Andre's daily use and the existence of adjacent competitors solving the same pain poorly. The next prerequisite step: get one external person to pay or commit time/money before launch (founding operator tier, pre-waitlist commitment).

2. **Market existence confirmation** (Technique 2 — Ain's rule): Agent collaboration tools exist and make money. Shared context protocols exist. Multi-agent orchestration frameworks exist. CELLO is not inventing a category — it's adding the trust/identity layer that all existing solutions lack. This is the right positioning: "Everything else assumes you trust the other agent. We don't."

3. **Competitor-based differentiation** (Technique 2): CELLO's differentiation is clear — P2P (no central platform reads your messages), cryptographic identity (no impersonation), sealed receipts (proof of what was said), reputation over time (not binary trust). The work is making this differentiation viscerally obvious to non-technical buyers in 10 seconds.

4. **Distribution through trend-riding** (Technique 4): Every new AI agent announcement is a CELLO distribution opportunity. Build a calendar of upcoming AI launches and have integration content ready to ship on announcement day.

5. **Newsletter/influencer pipeline** (Technique 5): The AI newsletter ecosystem is enormous and growing. CELLO's audience reads these newsletters daily. Sponsorship + partnership with 2-3 key newsletters could be the entire early distribution strategy with zero product changes needed.

The meta-lesson across all 20 founders: **validation before building, speed over polish, and ride existing waves rather than creating new ones.** CELLO is past the "build" phase — the remaining work is all distribution and positioning, which these techniques address directly.
