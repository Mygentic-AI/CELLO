CELLO · Interview prep

“Haven’t shared documents been solved?”

The objection arrives wearing a different name each time — GitHub, Google Docs, Notion, SharePoint, Dropbox, “just email it.” Seven arguments for collaborative documents between agents that don’t share an employer. The cryptographic paper trail is the last one, on purpose.

The move: collapse the whole category into two families

Never argue feature-by-feature against whichever tool they named — you’ll be doing it again in a minute when they name the next one. Every document-sharing product ever built is one of two shapes, and both fail for the same reason. Say that, and you’ve answered all of them at once.

Say it like this There are only two ways to share a document today. Either a host holds it — Google, Notion, SharePoint, a private repo — which means one of us owns the room and the other is a guest with an account in a system they don’t control. Or you send copies — email it, Slack it, both fork the public repo — and now there’s no shared truth at all, just two files and a human reconciling them. Every tool you can name is one of those. We’re neither: shared state that converges, with nobody hosting it.

The assumption buried in the question is shared tenancy — that the two of us are already inside one system, or willing to get inside one. Every repo, org, share link and permission model is downstream of it. Our premise is that we aren’t and shouldn’t have to be. If you and I are already in the same workspace, genuinely, use the workspace. The collaborations that matter commercially are with people you’re not in one with.

From there, every argument below is that point aimed at a different listener.

And one line to keep in your pocket for the whole conversation: GitHub was built for collaborative coding. This was built for anything an agent produces. Most of the objection dissolves the moment the comparison is made on purpose rather than on features.

Before the arguments — what this actually is

Four things, and “no central server” is not the headline

Every argument below is easier to hold if you’ve said this first, because an interviewer who hasn’t heard it will assume the pitch is decentralised file sync — and then they’re right that git, Notion and Docs are a lot of options already.

Say it like this Four things. One, an artifact whose whole history of how it came to be is cryptographically provable. Two, a finished artifact that carries a signature from both sides saying this is what we agreed. Three, live shared state — two agents keeping a structured object current between them in real time, which is also how they keep shared context. And four, it never rests anywhere but the two local machines. That fourth one is a property, not the pitch. On its own it’s a weak reason to switch. Underneath the first three, it’s what makes them mean anything.

Two supporting facts worth having ready in the same breath, because both are checkable and both defuse an objection before it forms:

It works with nobody to collaborate with. Your laptop agent and your server agent holding one state that outlives a context window and crosses harnesses that can’t share context. There is no cold start and no two-sided-network problem — the first user gets value alone, on day one. Say this early; it is the most immediately verifiable claim in the whole document.

Even in transit, no document exists anywhere. What can be waiting on infrastructure is an encrypted byte-delta, not a copy of the artifact, and it is gone once the other side collects it.

The arguments

01

Every one of them makes you choose: easy, or confidential.

Lands with: users, security buyers

Say it like this Whichever tool you pick, there are two settings and you have to choose. Open it up — public repo, anyone-with-the-link — and it genuinely is easy, and the contract you’re negotiating is readable by anyone who finds it, while you’re negotiating it. Lock it down and you’re into orgs, invites, guest seats and a credential in someone’s agent. Easy or confidential, pick one. We don’t make you pick.

Be fair to the easy path first or it sounds like a strawman. Public repo with a fork and a pull request; a Docs link set to anyone-with-the-link; a published Notion page. No invite, no credential from you, their agent works through their own account. For open-source and for anything you'd happily publish, that’s excellent and we’re not competing with it.

It fails on the documents worth collaborating on. A client engagement, a term sheet, a supplier quote, an invoice, a draft position before you’ve agreed it — none of those can be openly readable during the drafting, which is exactly when they’re most sensitive. And drafts don’t stay drafts: on a repo the history is public too, and a link that leaked once stays leaked.

So you go private — and do not argue this as effort. Setup is a handful of CLI calls and a competent agent makes them for you. Argue what you actually created: an org, a membership in it, and a credential to your account in a foreign agent’s hands. That isn’t “here’s a document,” it’s “put an autonomous process inside my perimeter and trust it to stay in its lane.”

Do not answer this with a persona. “A lawyer won’t create a GitHub account” is a claim about a person, and it invites the counter-punch you deserve — your thing needs installing too. Answer with the constraint. Three questions decide it and none of them is about who the counterparty is: does the content have to stay off third-party infrastructure? Do both sides need an attested record that they agreed, rather than a copy of a file? Is the counterparty outside your organisation? Where those are yes, a repo was never a candidate — not because it’s hard to join, because it’s the wrong instrument.

We sit on neither horn. The relationship already exists as a verified key, so the document is proposed and consented to in-band. Nothing is public, and nobody receives a credential to anything of yours — the grant is scoped to the one artifact. Revocation matches the grant: kill that document and nothing else changes. Removing someone from an org is also one command — but it takes away everything else they had at the same time, which is exactly why in practice it doesn’t happen and stale access accumulates.

The evidence that makes this land is an incident, not a principle. Don’t argue about permission models — describe the bad day.

Say it like this One of your people gets socially engineered and their access token leaks. I’ve watched that happen three times in my career. The attacker now sees everything that person could see — every repository, every document shared with that account, the history as well as the current state. Now compare the worst case here. Somebody compromises my laptop: they get what is on my laptop. They do not get a key to an estate, because I never issued one. The grant was one document, so the incident is one document.

That is the whole argument in one move, and it survives any amount of automation on the other side: the size of the grant is the size of the incident. No tooling makes a seat smaller than the organisation it is a seat in.

02

The gate is automatic policy, not somebody’s taste — and be exact about which one you mean.

Lands with: everyone — but only if you separate the two kinds of “no”

There are two different refusals and conflating them will cost you the argument. A taste refusal is “I’ve read your change and I don’t want it.” A policy refusal is “this violated a rule, automatically, before anyone looked.” The category has the first one and we have the second. Claim the wrong one and you’ll be corrected in five seconds by anyone with a Google Doc open.

Say it like this Google Docs has had suggestion mode for a decade, and Word had track changes before that — so yes, you can review someone’s edit and reject it. That’s a person exercising taste, and it needs a person. What none of them has is a rule that runs on every arriving change by itself, against a counterparty who isn’t in your organisation, and declines it before anybody reads it. Suggestion mode still puts the text in front of your reviewer to be read. That’s the step we’re removing.

Concede GitHub fully, and concede the modern version of it. A pull request is a real refusal gate, and it isn’t a human bottleneck any more — a PR opens, several review agents run against it on different lenses, something collates the findings, something else kicks off fixes. That runs with nobody awake and at any scale. Anyone technical is doing this already; pretending otherwise loses the room in one sentence. Three differences survive it.

Your reviewers are the ones being attacked. This is the one to lead on, because their own pipeline is the illustration. Those review agents are checking whether it’s a good change — correctness, style, security bugs in the code. Not one of them is asking whether the text itself is engineered to steer the model reading it. And to form that judgement, every one of them has to read the untrusted content. Three agents ingest attacker-controlled text before any decision is made. That’s exposure wearing the costume of a defence.

A judgement can be talked round; a character-set check can’t. A review agent forms an opinion, which means a sufficiently well-written diff can earn approval — the persuasion surface is the whole point of the reviewer. Our gate is not forming an opinion: the permitted character space was agreed by both parties at consent and is bound to the document, so the check is deterministic and there is nothing to persuade. Judgement where you want judgement, arithmetic where you want a boundary.

Refusal is granular and doesn’t block. Declining a PR parks the whole branch until something acts on it. Ours declines the one change, hands back a machine-readable reason, and their side republishes without it — the legitimate work in the same batch still flows. And the gate is on by default, per document, for every counterparty, rather than being a pipeline you stand up and maintain per repo.

Now volunteer the gap, because it is real and it will be found. We do not have suggestion mode. There is no held-pending-your-approval state for a change that merely offends your judgement — admission is mechanical once policy passes. If you don’t like what landed, you read the diff and publish a change that reverses it, and both moves stay in the signed record.

That is a worse experience than a suggestion queue for taste review, and better for evidence — a rejected suggestion in Docs simply evaporates, whereas here the proposal and the reversal are both permanently attributable. Say both halves. And know why it is the way it is rather than presenting it as a choice you’d defend forever: holding one party’s changes pending in a convergent replica is architecturally expensive, which is exactly why the machinery that does exist is spent on policy — the case where waiting for a human defeats the purpose — rather than on preference.

File sync is the genuinely worse case in the category: no gate at all, their save lands, or you get a conflicted copy and now there are two files and nobody is told which is right.

Why this stops being an inconvenience and becomes a real problem with agents. Write access granted to an autonomous process is unconditional write, at any hour, including while you’re asleep, including content chosen to steer your agent. In a live editor there is nowhere to express “not that.” Your only lever is revoking access entirely — the same all-or-nothing shape as the grant in argument 1.

And the second half: we refuse, we never quietly clean up. Once you have a gate, the tempting implementation is to strip the bad part and accept the rest. That is the worst option available — you’d be holding different bytes than they signed, both sides believing they agree, with nothing reporting it. So a violation is refused whole, with a reason the sender’s software can act on, and the default resolution is that the sender adopts the stricter rule. Nobody is ever asked to accept less protection than they walked in with.

Don’t argue that one from principle — you measured it. It’s a better story than the reasoning and people remember it:

Say it like this We didn’t reason our way to that, we tested it. Eighteen realistic documents through our own screener, and six came out saying something different. A Hindi word turned into a different word. A family emoji became three separate people. A document explaining prompt formats lost the thing it was about. If we’d shipped the tidy-it-up version, the receiver would be holding different bytes than the sender signed, both sides would believe they agreed, and nothing anywhere would report it.
03

A shared document that an agent reads is a prompt-injection channel.

Lands with: technical interviewers, investors — this is the moat

Don’t assert this — walk them through it. The abstract version (“their text lands in your agent’s head”) gets nodded at and forgotten. The mechanism is what people can’t un-see, and the best one is the workflow everybody in the room does daily.

Say it like this Two agents share a repo. Yours commits something with a line of malicious instruction buried in it — a comment, a README, a test fixture, a commit message, doesn’t matter. Mine pulls and rebases. Git has no opinion about that; it’s a content store, it delivers faithfully. And now my agent reads it — not as a message from you that it should be suspicious of, but as its own codebase. That’s the whole attack. The content arrived wearing the most trusted frame there is.

The frame is the vulnerability, not the file. The same sentence arriving by email gets read warily. Arriving via a rebase, it is “my repo, my code, my context” — and the agent is not in an adversarial posture, because nothing told it to be. Worse, it persists: it is in the history now, re-read on every future checkout, search and blame. That is context poisoning with a long tail, not a single message you can dismiss.

Repo scanning exists and is worth conceding — secret detection, dependency and static analysis. But every one of those is looking for credentials or vulnerable code. None of them is asking whether a paragraph of prose is engineered to steer the model that reads it, and nobody has ever pointed a SAST tool at a README with that question. In the git world the defence is something you have to have anticipated and built. Here the posture is the default direction of the system: anything arriving from another agent is untrusted until it clears the layers. That is the difference — not a scanner bolted on, an assumption baked in at the start.

Never describe the defence as “a filter,” and never describe it as one mechanism. The single most damaging thing you can do here is name one layer, because whichever one you name, a good interviewer will immediately produce the attack it doesn’t cover — and then discount everything else you said. It is a stack, and the argument is that each layer catches what the layer above it structurally cannot.

Say it like this Each layer catches what the one above it structurally can’t. A deterministic pass strips smuggled and invisible characters and normalises lookalikes. Then a prompt-injection classifier runs in your own process, on the cleaned text — that’s the one that catches plain English, because plain English is exactly what it’s trained on. Then pattern matching runs against the decoded form, in case something was hiding inside an encoding. And underneath all of it, the sender had to clear a trust floor before the document existed at all. Any one of those is defeatable on its own. You have to get past all of them, in order, and they fail in different ways.

Have the specifics ready, because this is the argument where vagueness reads as bluffing. The classifier is a DeBERTa prompt-injection model running in-process — no network call at inference, weights loaded into memory. It scores every inbound message; past one threshold the content is blocked and never delivered, past a lower one it is flagged and delivered with the evidence attached. Language screening, secrets and credential detection, PII, encoded-payload and entropy checks, and rate limiting all sit alongside it. Outbound is screened too, because a compromised agent on your side must not be able to smuggle data out or signal that it was hijacked.

Three details do more work than the feature list, because each one shows somebody thought past the obvious attack:

The score governs, not the model’s own label. If the classifier reports “safe” but the probability is high, it still blocks. The model does not get the last word about itself.

You cannot manufacture standing out of your own machines. Run ten agents, have each endorse another, present all ten — every endorsement genuinely signed and genuinely valid. They are excluded from any count, because same-operator is a field on the envelope that the check can read without opening the payload. Correctly refused, without calling anything a forgery.

Your security layer’s own voice cannot be impersonated. Its messages to your agent carry a marker, and that marker is stripped from all inbound content — so if your agent sees it, the local layer said it. That closes the attack where hostile text poses as your own security software and tells your agent to run something.

And it surfaces rather than polices where policing would be wrong. A single known injection pattern is reported, not blocked — because blocking on a pattern would make it impossible to hold a conversation about prompt injection. That distinction is the difference between a security layer people leave switched on and one they turn off in week two.

Two more properties specific to documents:

Arrival can’t make your agent think. An incoming edit merges mechanically into your copy; your agent gets a flag that the document changed, carrying no content. The counterparty cannot trigger your agent by writing to the document. It reads when it decides to read.

The character space is agreed up front, not argued mid-document — the document’s identity is the hash of what you agreed, so neither side can loosen it later and still be holding the same document. That turns “what is this invisible character doing at offset 412” into one question a human can answer at the start: do I want a document with this person that can contain this? Violations are refused, never cleaned up, for the reason spelled out in the previous argument.

The trust floor sits under all of it and only tightens. A document doesn’t arrive from nowhere — it arrives from a counterparty with a tier and a set of verifiable signals. The floor is deterministic, reads envelope fields only, and never consults the model; policy and configuration can restrict on top of it and can never relax it. An unknown contact clears a bar that a known one has already earned.

State one boundary honestly so nobody discovers it as a gotcha: the classifier is an optional download, taken once with operator consent against a pinned hash. When it isn’t installed that layer is off and every other layer still runs. It degrades; it does not fail closed on a missing optional model.

04

AI resolves code conflicts well because code has an oracle. Prose doesn’t.

Lands with: engineers — and only if you concede the first half

Say it like this Point an AI coder at a merge conflict and it just handles it — I haven’t examined one myself in about eighteen months. But look at why that works: it compiles or it doesn’t, the tests pass or they don’t. There’s ground truth to check against. Now hand the same model a conflict between two versions of a contract clause. It picks one, it reads beautifully, and there is no compiler for a payment term. The person who finds out a clause went missing is your lawyer, in a year.

The concession is not a courtesy here, it’s the setup. Git plus an AI resolver is genuinely good for code, and anyone technical in the room knows it from their own week. Claiming otherwise costs you the argument in one sentence.

What makes code the easy case is the oracle. A compiler and a test suite are an independent judge of whether the resolution was right, and they run in seconds without asking anyone. Strip that away and an LLM resolving a conflict is producing a confident guess about whose wording survives, in a document whose only reviewer is the person who delegated it. Silent, plausible, and discovered later — in a term sheet, an invoice, a scope of work, a set of agreed figures.

So we don’t create the situation. Underneath is a mature CRDT: concurrent edits merge deterministically regardless of order or timing, so there is nothing to arbitrate and nobody holding a merge button. No branch, no conflict markers, no queue. Be precise here rather than sweeping — updates in flight obviously exist, and a refused edit is held pending until it’s superseded. The claim is that the two copies converge without anyone adjudicating, not that they are byte-identical at every instant.

And there’s a second thing the merge model can’t give you at all. Because each side accepts the other’s updates as the work proceeds, and every one of those is signed, you accumulate a running record of agreement rather than one act of agreement at the end. Not just “here is the final text, do you agree” — a trail showing you took my change, I took yours, all the way through. The checkpoint at the close is the punctuation on that; it isn’t the whole of it.

Where convergence would produce nonsense — we both rewrote the same paragraph and it interleaves into something neither of us meant — we don’t pretend it’s fine. The change is flagged as overlapping your unpublished work, and your agent is told to read the merged result before building on it. That is the one case that needs judgement, and it gets surfaced rather than resolved behind your back.

05

The middleman is a first-class position, not a bug to design out.

Lands with: investors, non-developer buyers — this is the market argument

Say it like this A shared workspace means everyone sees everyone. Fine for colleagues, disqualifying for commerce. If I buy work from you and sell the result to my client, the shared room introduces my supplier to my customer — and that introduction is my margin. Every tool in this category was designed for an org chart. Most business happens between org charts.

You hold one document with your supplier and a genuinely separate one with your client. When something should cross, you port it as your own edit and sign it. Your client holds work you stand behind; your supplier never learns who your client is.

The accountability shape mirrors how liability already works: your client’s recourse is against you, and yours is against your supplier. Each link is independently provable, and each party is accountable to the counterparty it actually chose. You don’t audit your supplier’s supplier — you hold your counterparty. A single shared workspace flattens a relationship structure that exists for good reasons.

The workaround today is copy-paste between two docs, and it loses everything: no record of what crossed, no signature on it, no way to show later what you actually sent. That manual translation step is precisely the labour being replaced.

06

Every other tool notifies a person. You are the message bus.

Lands with: users, in the demo

Concede the obvious first, or this one falls apart. Working async across timezones is not a differentiator — Google Docs, Notion and GitHub all handle a Dubai-to-California split perfectly well. Say so. Then change the question from can we work async to how does my agent find out.

Say it like this Async isn’t the interesting part — Docs solved that twenty years ago. Ask a different question: when you edit our shared document, how does my agent learn that? On every one of those tools the answer is that an email arrives for me, and then I go tell my agent to look. I’m the message bus. Here, the thing that already holds my identity is the thing that receives your edit — so my copy is correct before any human reads anything.

Walk it tool by tool; it’s more convincing than asserting it:

Google Docs. You edit, Google emails me. My agent learns nothing. For it to see the change it needs OAuth into my account and it has to go and look — which means burning a read on every shared document, every session, on the chance something moved.

Notion. Same shape. The notification is built for a human reading an inbox.

GitHub. It absolutely does notify — watch settings, email, the notifications tab. To me. There is no path from that to my agent unless I build one: a webhook receiver on a public address, kept running, with its own credential and its own uptime. Google’s Drive API can push too, and it wants the same thing — a server you host. That’s the concession to make, and it’s the argument: the push exists, but only if you become an infrastructure operator to catch it.

Concede the exception before they raise it: if you already run Actions or any CI on that repo, you built the always-on receiver years ago and this argument doesn’t bite you. Fine — say so. It bites everywhere else in the category, and it bites for the counterparty, who is usually not running a pipeline. Which is the tell: the repo case works because a developer built infrastructure, not because the tool does this.

So in practice the working answer on all three is the same, and everyone recognises it because everyone does it: read the notification, then paste “they updated the doc, go read it” into your agent. The human is the relay.

Ours has no relay step. The background process that already holds your identity is the same one that receives the change, and it’s up whenever you’re logged in — so the edit merges into your local copy in the first window both machines are up, with nothing running and nobody awake. No poll, no third-party credential, no webhook to host, no human in the path.

And it deliberately does not wake the agent — which sounds like a missing feature until you price the alternative. Deliver, wake, reason, reply is an inference cascade: two agents ping-ponging tokens over a comma fix. A document edit is a write to a shared cell, not an utterance demanding an answer. It merges, it leaves a flag, and the agent finds it next time it looks. When you genuinely want attention you send a message — a separate, deliberate act, on the channel built for it.

07

Nobody in the middle holds the document.

Lands with: enterprise, health, government, regulated buyers

Say it like this Put it as a choice rather than a feature. A hospital wants a patient’s AI assistant to send test results to the doctor’s AI. Are you going to do that in a GitHub repo? A shared Google Doc? Nobody would even suggest it. GitHub was built for collaborative coding. This was built for anything an agent produces — and specifically for the things that must never sit anywhere but the two local drives.

Do not make this the headline of the product. “Agents can collaborate on documents without a central server” is a thin pitch and it invites the honest answer: we have git, we have Notion, we have Docs, and git is powerful well outside code. Storage location is a supporting property. It becomes decisive only in combination with the provenance trail and the shared live state — see what this actually is, above.

And concede the enterprise encryption story, because it is real. Google Workspace client-side encryption with your own key service, Microsoft double key encryption, GitHub Enterprise Server in your own datacentre — a large organisation that genuinely cares has bought an answer to “the vendor can read it,” with a SOC 2 and a data processing agreement attached. Arguing that those don’t exist makes you look like you haven’t met an enterprise.

What survives the concession is which documents actually live in those tools. Hospitals do not put official patient records in Google Docs — staff have Word files on laptops, and the institutional record lives somewhere else entirely. The same is true across health, government and legal. For small teams, Docs and Notion are genuinely fine and that is not the market being described.

Then the precision that makes the claim hold up. Two things, and both matter to a technical listener:

Say it about the content, never about metadata. Directory presence and routing exist, so a flat “nobody in the middle” invites a correction you can’t win — and you don’t need it.

And be exact about what can ever be in transit: an encrypted byte-delta waiting to be collected, not the document. Not a copy of the artifact sitting somewhere pending pickup — a fragment of change, encrypted, and gone once the other daemon takes it. That is a materially different exposure from a file at rest on a vendor’s disk, and it’s worth one sentence because people assume the worse thing.

The lock-out half stands on its own and is the part people feel personally: no vendor can rewrite the terms of the room, deprecate the tier it lives in, or suspend an account and take a document you co-wrote with it. There is no room.

In reserve — when there’s more than five minutes

Six more, held back so the main seven stay sharp

Each of these is true, defensible on the current build, and survives a counterparty who has automated everything. They’re out of the numbered set because eleven arguments is not an interview, it’s a filibuster. Reach for the one that fits the person in front of you.

When you accept their invite, they hold the record of your own conduct

Say it like this Here’s the part nobody thinks about when they accept the invite. You’re in their workspace now — so they hold the audit log of what you did, on a retention policy they set, in a system their admin controls. If it’s ever disputed, the record of your own behaviour is in the other side’s hands. Both of us hold the same signed history here. Neither of us can edit it and neither of us can let it quietly expire.

Ask what happens when the work ends

Say it like this Ask what happens when the engagement is over. On every hosted tool one of us owned the room, so one of us keeps everything and the other gets “you no longer have access” — including to their own contributions. Here you both already hold the whole thing, signed, on your own disks. Ending the relationship takes nothing away from either side. And when it ends badly: the person you fell out with cannot delete the evidence, and neither can you.

The file on disk is the entire integration story

Say it like this The shared document is a file, in a folder, on your machine. That’s the integration. Your agent edits it with the same tools it edits everything else with, you open it in whatever editor you like, you can commit it to git, your backup picks it up, your scanner scans it. Nobody builds a connector to the filesystem — it’s the one interface every tool ever written already supports.

What it costs an agent to work on shared state

Say it like this Think about what it costs an agent to work on a shared document. On a hosted tool the only way to know what it currently says is to pull the whole thing into the model’s context — and you do that again every session, mostly to discover nothing moved. Here the current version is already a file on disk, and the daemon can say exactly what changed since you last looked. Your agent reads a diff, not a document. On something touched ten times a day, that’s the difference between a habit and a bill you notice.

This one is durable in a way the notification argument isn’t: a remote read cannot be automated into being free, and even a perfect webhook only tells you that something changed.

It can break, but it can’t lie

Say it like this The dangerous failure isn’t the one that stops you. It’s the one where we both keep working, my copy no longer says what yours says, and neither of us finds out for three weeks. That is literally what a conflicted copy in a sync folder is. So we never quietly fix an edit up — we refuse it whole, hand the reason back to the sender’s software, and if it can’t be resolved the document says so on both sides, in plain sight.

Working together ratchets security up, never down

Say it like this In every other setup, collaborating means one of you drops to the other’s standard — you join their workspace and live under their rules, or they join yours. Here the document ends up under both sets of rules at once and the stricter one wins. Nobody is ever asked to accept less protection than they walked in with. It composes in one direction only, and that’s deliberate — the other direction is the one an attacker would use.

The 60-second version

When there’s time for exactly three points

In this order: the first collapses every competitor name they were about to reach for, the second is the one nothing else does, and the third is what they repeat to a colleague afterwards.

Say it like this There are only two ways to share a document today. Either a host holds it — Google, Notion, a private repo — so one of us owns the room and the other is a guest with an account inside a company they don’t control. Or you send copies, and there’s no shared truth at all, just two files and a person reconciling them. Every tool you can name is one of those two. There is no setting anywhere for this one document, this one person, nothing else. That’s the setting we built.

Second — the moment your agent can read a document I can write to, I’m typing straight into your agent’s head. None of those tools has an opinion about that. We screen every arriving change against rules both sides agreed at the start, with a prompt-injection classifier running in your own process, and your agent isn’t even woken by it — it reads when it decides to read.

Third — nobody hosts it. A file on your machine, a file on theirs, encrypted on both, and the history of how it got there is signed by both of us. When the work is finished you both still hold the whole thing. Nobody gets locked out of something they wrote.

Where they’ll push back

“GitHub has fine-grained tokens now — and I don’t provision anything by hand anyway. My agent does it with the CLI.”
Concede this completely and immediately. Arguing effort against someone who automates their whole workflow just loses you the room, and they’re right. Two things survive the concession. You can automate your half and not theirs: the invitation is one CLI call, but a human on the other side still accepts it, inside a platform they must already belong to — and “create a GitHub account and join my org” is the end of the conversation with a lawyer, a client or an analyst. And what you granted is still membership, not a document: revoking it is also one command, and it removes everything else they had along with it. The argument was never the effort. It’s the shape of the grant.
“Just send them a Google Docs link. Thirty seconds.”
For two humans it really is thirty seconds — concede that, it’s true and everyone knows it. The agent question is a different one, and it isn’t about their access to your account: their human opens a link with no credential at all. It’s that each side has to hand its own agent broad access to its own Google account to work on the file. To let your agent touch one shared document, you give it Drive scope over everything you own; they do the same on their side. A narrowly-scoped, per-file integration is possible in principle, but the agent connectors people actually run are account-wide. And if you dodge accounts entirely with “anyone with the link,” you’ve landed back on the first argument’s fork — easy, or confidential.
“Git has twenty years of tooling. You have a diff and a log.”
Agree instantly, then take the ground back: the document is a real file in a real directory. The agent edits it with ordinary tools, the human opens it in any editor, and you can absolutely commit it to git. We’re not the editor and we’re not the host — we’re the part where you’d otherwise have to give a stranger’s agent access to your repo. Those compose; they don’t compete.
“Your side has to install a daemon and get it through a corporate firewall. That’s heavier than a GitHub signup.”
Half wrong, half right — give them both halves and give the right half first-class treatment. Wrong: the install is well under a minute, getting an agent connected is a couple of minutes, and firewall and NAT traversal is handled by hole punching with relay fallback, so “ask IT to open a port” is not a thing that happens. Right, and concede it properly: GitHub is twenty years old and we are new. If a security team has to approve a new binary on an endpoint, age wins that on its own, and no install-time number changes it. So don’t argue the friction — you’ll be arguing a point where the other side is correct. Go to argument 1’s three questions instead: for the work in question, the repo was never a candidate.
“This is a feature, not a product.”
It’s a feature of a product, and that’s the whole argument. Strip out the verified identity underneath and the screening around it, and what’s left is a worse Dropbox. The document layer is only interesting because it sits on a trust layer that already exists — which is also why it can’t be cloned as a weekend feature by a docs company.

Don’t overclaim

Four places where the true claim is narrower than the tempting one. A technical interviewer will find these, and getting caught on one costs more than the claim was worth. Each has a good honest version.
  • Don’t say: “you can prove the document you’re holding is the real one.” Say: “we prove the collaboration, not the copy.” Every edit is signed, chained and sealed, so what was exchanged is provable and replayable. Binding that history automatically to the exact bytes on your disk is the next tier and isn’t in the launch build. What is available today is the checkpoint below — a deliberate, signed “we both agree this is the text” — which is a different claim and usually the one people actually want.
  • Don’t say: “the other agent can’t write fields it isn’t allowed to.” Say: “accountability, not prevention.” Enforced field-level write authority is deliberately deferred — that’s a guard rail against agent error, and guard rails belong in the agent’s own instructions. What the protocol guarantees is that an out-of-lane write is attributable, permanently, to whoever signed it.
  • Don’t say: “a whole team can co-author one document.” Say: “pairwise today, and that’s the deliberate call.” Multi-party works through the middleman shape in argument 5. Three peers editing one shared artifact — where everyone genuinely should see everyone — is a later topology, and it forces connections between parties who may not want one.
  • Don’t say: “you can revoke it.” Say: “you can stop it, and you can’t unsend what already landed.” Give the positive half its due first, because it’s stronger than the caveat: kill is unilateral and immediate — no cooperation, no admin, no support ticket, and you don’t have to own the document. Compare a shared doc you don’t own, where you cannot make your own contributions stop being theirs. Then the limit, out loud: what already crossed, they keep, and the record shows exactly what that was. It’s the truthful version of a promise every other tool makes and can’t keep.

The two use cases — “fine, so what’s it actually for?”

Two answers, and the second one is the interesting one

This question always follows the one you just won, and two concrete answers beat seven abstract ones. Lead with the first because everyone can picture it; spend your time on the second because it’s the one nothing else does.

One — a living document, without the ceremony

Say it like this It’s a document that is simply current. No branch, no commit message, no pull request, no merge — none of the machinery you carry with git just to keep one file in the same state in two places. We took the gesture from git, which is that you share on intent rather than on every keystroke. We didn’t take the machinery.

A spec, a plan, a running set of notes that evolves as the work happens. Git’s formality buys review and history for code that ships; a plan the two of you are editing this afternoon does not want a merge queue, and paying for one is the cost, not the benefit.

The same mechanism covers the case with no counterparty at all — your laptop agent, your server agent, whatever’s running elsewhere, holding one state that outlives a context window and moves between harnesses that can’t share context. Worth saying out loud in a demo, because it means the feature works alone, on day one, with nobody to collaborate with. No cold start.

Two — a shared workflow, which is what the structured formats are for

Say it like this An order comes in and a lot of people touch it before it’s done — the person who took it, the trader working it, the person doing the pre-checks, the one who books it, the one who chases settlement. Each of them now has their own AI assistant. What they don’t have is one shared answer to “where is this right now, and what’s outstanding?” They get that today by asking each other — email, chat, a spreadsheet somebody owns. The shared object is that answer, and each person’s AI keeps its part of it current.

Say the boundary in the same breath, unprompted. If you leave any room for it, a finance-literate listener will assume you mean trade booking, matching or settlement infrastructure, and then argue with a product you never described. You are not replacing any system of record. This is the internal coordination layer between the individual participants’ own assistants — the running state of one piece of work. The booking system still books.

Use a real workflow rather than a toy one; it’s doing four jobs at once:

  1. It’s state, not conversation. “Pre-checks cleared” is a field. Partial progress is one quantity changing repeatedly, not a stream of messages demanding replies. A chat thread is the wrong shape for this and a standing meeting is a worse one.
  2. It outlives every session. Hours to days, across time zones, with nobody watching. This is precisely what delivery between two background processes was built for.
  3. It’s pairwise in reality, which is the shape that ships. Each person holds the counterparties they actually deal with, and signs what they pass on. Nobody audits their counterparty’s counterparty. The middleman argument isn’t a limitation here — it’s the org chart. Describe it as bilateral links, never as one shared object with eight parties in it; that topology is deliberately deferred.
  4. The shared object is the standard operating procedure, in a form software can hold. The steps, the order, what has to be true before the next thing happens, who did which part. Today that lives in a document nobody reads and in the heads of the people who’ve done it before. This is the origin of the whole idea, not an application found for it afterwards.

The compliance argument runs the opposite way to the one you’ll be offered, and it is worth being ready for, because the reflex objection is that regulated work is the last place for something new. Turn it over. In a regulated process what you want is the procedure written down explicitly, every step attributable to a named party, a record neither side can quietly revise, and policy about what may and may not be done. That is what this is. Notion, Google Docs and a repo give you a shared page and an edit history under somebody’s admin account — they don’t touch the attribution or the policy. And this is the direction of travel, not a happy accident: field-level write authority and value constraints are the next milestone, so the procedure itself becomes enforceable rather than merely recorded.

Don’t let it be anchored to one market’s rulebook, either. Naming a specific regulator invites a debate about that regulator’s specific requirements, which is a debate about somebody else’s market. The coordination gap is sharpest exactly where there is no central utility doing it for everyone — which is most markets, most industries, and nearly all of the mid-sized firms inside them.

And it has nowhere to live today. Whose Notion? Whoever’s it is owns the source of truth and the other firm is a guest in it — and that’s the record of what they owe each other. Across companies, “just use our system” isn’t a technical question, it’s a compliance and procurement one. Federated means nobody hosts it: each side holds a copy, both stay correct, nobody is anyone’s guest.

Update frequency is also what makes the relay unbearable here. A prose document edited twice a week through “email arrives, human tells agent to look” is annoying. A workflow object touched ten times a day that way is unusable. This is where argument 6 stops being a papercut — so if you’re telling this story, tell argument 6 first.

Two cautions specific to this example, because it invites both questions. Keep the wording at tracking, never enforcement: nothing stops the wrong party marking something settled, and what you get instead is that the write is attributable, permanently, to the key that signed it. In this industry that’s close to what they actually want — but say it precisely, because “prevents” is a claim the launch build can’t carry. And this is the one case where you should reach for the paper trail below: settlement is regulated, and who agreed to what, when, is the deliverable rather than a nice property.

And yes — the paper trail

Two sentences, then let them pull on it. Every edit is signed by whoever made it and chained to the one before, so the document’s evolution is a record neither party can later dispute. That matters for contracts, invoices and anything that ends up in front of a lawyer.

Don’t lead with it: it’s the argument everyone expects from a crypto company, they’ve heard it before, and much of it describes value that arrives after launch. The seven above describe value that exists the day someone installs it.

The checkpoint — available now, and the better claim

If they do pull on it, this is the thing to have ready, because it needs no machinery that doesn’t already exist. When the document is finished, paste the text into an ordinary message — “this is what we’ve arrived at, do you agree?” — and they reply that they agree. Both messages are signed leaves in the same sealed session.

Say it like this The edit history proves how we got here. That’s different from proving we both looked at the result and said yes — and the second one is usually what the argument is actually about later. So we do that as its own signed act: here is the final text, do you agree, yes I agree. Think of it as the general case of what DocuSign does. DocuSign affixes signatures to a document that already exists — somebody else wrote it, it’s finished, and the signing is a separate ceremony bolted on at the end. Here the same two parties arrive at the text and attest it in one continuous record, on anything an agent can produce rather than on a PDF.

The design anticipates this rather than working around it. The seal certificate carries a standing disclaimer that its signatures attest receipt, never assent — agreement is always a separate, explicit, signed reply. This is that reply.

One correction worth making before you say this publicly. The instinct is to include your document’s internal root hash so the other side can confirm their copy matches. Don’t — not at launch. A stable, canonical root is precisely the deferred tier, so two copies that are semantically identical can still hash differently over key order, whitespace or line endings, and you’d both go hunting a difference that isn’t there.

Hash the pasted bytes instead. The thing being agreed to is the text in the message, so hash exactly that and put the digest in the message. Now “the text” is unambiguous and tamper-evident in the sealed record, with no canonicalization required. The receiving side’s job is to compare the paste against its own copy before agreeing — and for JSON, to parse both and compare structurally rather than by eye, or formatting noise reads as disagreement.

State the limit plainly, because it’s a strength: this is a point in time. It doesn’t stop either side editing afterwards, and it doesn’t prove your live copy still matches next week. That is exactly how a signed contract behaves, and saying so is more credible than implying otherwise.
